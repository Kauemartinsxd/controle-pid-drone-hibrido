\begin{abstract}
This article compares two flight control laws --- a classical cascade PID
and an output-feedback linear--quadratic regulator (LQRy) --- for the
fixed-wing flight mode of a small hybrid vertical take-off and landing
(VTOL) UAV, following the complete path from design to embedded
validation. Both controllers are designed on a model linearized about the
cruise trim of a nonlinear model identified from flight-test data, whose
open-loop dynamics include an unstable spiral mode, and are evaluated
against a common set of literature-grounded closed-loop requirements. In
software-in-the-loop (SIL) simulation on the nonlinear model, the two
laws are exercised under identical references on a 300-second combined
mission; the gain-scheduled LQRy tracks faster and with smaller RMS
errors but violates the heading-overshoot requirement, while the
requirements-retuned PID meets every requirement at lower bandwidth. A
robustness study then reveals complementary weaknesses: under a severe
lateral--directional model mismatch both compared laws lose the
mission --- the aggressive LQRy diverging outright, while an earlier,
softer fixed-gain LQRy iteration completes it with near-nominal error,
showing that this robustness is set by the tuning rather than by the
LQ structure --- whereas under a
longitudinal-only mismatch the LQRy stays nominal and the PID's
clamped altitude loop drifts; under wind and gusts the LQRy concedes
altitude where the PID stays firm; both tolerate a plausible $+10\%$
identification uncertainty. Finally, both controllers are deployed on an
STM32 microcontroller through automatic code generation and compared on a
lockstep serial hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) bench that is shown to be
bit-deterministic, with, for the synchronized PID deployment, a SIL--HIL
residual at the quantization floor of the bus, and a sensitivity study
ranking the implementation choices that actually affect embedded
agreement. The complete toolchain and data are
publicly available.
\end{abstract}
